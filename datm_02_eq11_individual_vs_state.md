# Justice: Essential Question 11
## *When the state and the individual want different things, whose needs take precedence — and can a state that overrides the individual still claim to guarantee freedom?*

### Insert after EQ 8 — Pre-Text Contextual Inquiry for *Death and the Maiden*

---

**Core Socio-Cultural Tension:** The fundamental paradox of liberal democratic governance. The state derives its authority from the consent of individuals — the social contract — yet routinely subordinates individual needs to collective imperatives: stability, unity, economic viability, international standing. In post-authoritarian contexts, this tension becomes acute. Gerardo's Commission is, on its face, a mechanism of individual redress; in practice, it is a political instrument designed to manage collective transition. Paulina's need — full acknowledgement, named accountability, experienced justice — is precisely what the state cannot afford to provide without destabilising the fragile order Gerardo has been appointed to protect. The state offers her process; she needs truth. These are not the same thing, and the play knows it.

The deeper implication is philosophical: the social contract (Rousseau, Locke, Hobbes) promises individuals that surrendering certain freedoms to the state will result in protection, rights, and the guarantee of justice. But what happens when the state has been the instrument of violation? The contract has already been broken — by torture chambers that operated under state authority, by courts that looked away, by commissions that now ask survivors to exchange full justice for partial acknowledgement. The very institution offering Paulina justice is constituted by the same order that permitted her abuse. She is being asked to trust the state again. The play asks: on what grounds could she possibly do so?

This tension maps directly onto the three characters. Gerardo *is* the state — appointed, procedural, committed to institutional legitimacy. Roberto Miranda embodies the state's self-protective interest: his freedom is the price the state has implicitly agreed to pay for stability. Paulina is the individual whose needs the state cannot fully accommodate without threatening its own coherence. Her act of extra-legal justice is, in this reading, not madness or vengeance but a logical response to the state's structural inability to deliver what she requires.

## Development Strategies

Introduce the foundational concept of the social contract through a brief, accessible framing activity. Present students with this proposition: *You agree to give up certain freedoms — the freedom to take private revenge, to ignore laws you disagree with, to act purely on personal conviction — in exchange for the state's protection and its commitment to justice on your behalf. What does the state owe you in return? And what happens if it fails to deliver?* Allow students to identify where they stand before any philosophical scaffolding is introduced.

Then introduce the three classic theorists as distinct positions: Hobbes (the state as necessary constraint on destructive individual freedom — without it, life is "nasty, brutish and short"), Locke (the state as servant of individual rights — when it fails those rights, resistance is legitimate), and Rousseau (the general will — individuals must sometimes subordinate personal interests to what the collective decides is good). Ask students to identify which character in *Death and the Maiden* most closely embodies each position, then consider whether any of these theorists anticipated the problem Paulina faces.

As a consolidating activity, pose the following structured scenario: A post-authoritarian commission offers perpetrators amnesty in exchange for testimony — truth, but no punishment. Survivors are told this is the price of national stability. In small groups, students must argue: (a) why a survivor should accept this; (b) why a survivor would be morally justified in refusing it; (c) whether the state has the right to make this demand of its citizens. The aim is not resolution but productive confrontation with the irresolvable.

## Guiding Questions for Progression

- If the state's legitimacy rests on its guarantee of justice, does it lose that legitimacy when it fails to deliver justice to an individual? Or is legitimacy always a collective, not individual, matter?
- Gerardo believes that working within the system — however imperfect — is the only responsible path. Paulina acts outside it. Who is the more responsible citizen?
- Is it possible to be simultaneously loyal to a state and in fundamental conflict with what that state can or will do for you? What does that feel like — and who is most often asked to bear that contradiction?
- The Commission investigates only cases that ended in death, may not publicly name those responsible, and works under an amnesty. It offers acknowledgement without consequence. Is acknowledgement without consequence a form of justice, or a form of institutional self-protection dressed as justice?
- If freedom means anything, must it include the freedom to refuse the state's version of justice — even at personal and social cost?

## Additional Vocabulary for This Tension

**Social contract** — the implicit agreement between individuals and the state whereby individuals surrender certain liberties in exchange for protection, rights, and justice. When the state violates the contract — as an authoritarian regime does systematically — the basis for individual compliance with state authority becomes philosophically untenable. In *Death and the Maiden*, the Commission represents an attempt to renew the contract, but renewal requires survivors to bear disproportionate costs.

**Sovereignty** — the supreme authority of the state over individuals and territory. Authoritarian regimes assert absolute sovereignty, including over bodies. The torture enacted on Paulina was an act of sovereign power. Her counter-assertion of control in Act Two is, on one reading, a reclamation of personal sovereignty — the individual refusing to remain a subject of state authority.

**Civil disobedience** — deliberate, non-violent refusal to comply with unjust laws or institutional directives, on the grounds that a higher moral law supersedes them. Paulina's actions complicate this category: she is not non-violent, nor is she refusing to comply with a law. She is acting in the absence of law, in the space where law has failed.

**Amnesty** — formal pardon, typically granted by a state to individuals for political offences. Distinguished from forgiveness (personal) and from exoneration (finding of innocence). Amnesty is a political act: it prioritises stability over accountability. Its implication is that the state has decided the cost of prosecution exceeds the benefit — a calculation made on behalf of all citizens, including those most harmed.

**Liberal democracy** — a system of governance combining majority rule with protection of individual rights and liberties. The central promise is that the state will not use its power against individuals in certain ways. *Death and the Maiden* is set in a society attempting to become one, and interrogates whether the transition is genuine or cosmetic.

**State violence** — harm enacted or permitted by state institutions: police brutality, military torture, enforced disappearances, judicial execution. Distinct from private violence in that it carries institutional authority and social legitimacy. The play's horror lies partly in the fact that what was done to Paulina was not aberrant — it was policy.

**Impunity (structural)** — when systems, not just individuals, guarantee freedom from consequence for powerful actors. Miranda's potential freedom is not merely a personal outcome; it is a structural feature of how transitional justice mechanisms have been designed.

**The private and the political** — feminist political theory's critique of the distinction between public (political, institutionally significant) and private (personal, domestic, individually experienced). Paulina's rape was a political act enacted on a private body. The Commission treats political violence as its remit; sexual torture occupies an ambiguous and historically marginalised category.
