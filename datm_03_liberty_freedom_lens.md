# Conceptual Lens: Liberty, Freedom, and the Just Society
### A Framework for Reading the Individual vs. State Tension Through the Problem of Freedom
#### Supplementary Framework for *Death and the Maiden*

---

## Framing the Lens

Justice and freedom are habitually invoked together, as though they are natural allies — two pillars of the same liberal democratic ideal. *Death and the Maiden* systematically dismantles this assumption. It stages a society in the act of claiming to become free and just simultaneously, and it asks, with mounting discomfort, whether either claim is credible when examined from the position of those most harmed.

The lens developed here does not treat liberty and freedom as synonyms for a vague political good. It insists on precision — because the distinctions between different *kinds* of freedom are exactly what the play's characters are fighting about, often without realising it. Gerardo and Paulina do not simply want different outcomes. They are operating with fundamentally different conceptions of what freedom means and what justice requires in order to produce it. Understanding this philosophically transforms every scene.

---

## The Core Distinction: Negative vs. Positive Liberty

Isaiah Berlin's influential distinction provides the essential conceptual architecture.

**Negative liberty** is freedom *from* interference — the absence of coercion, constraint, or obstruction by external agents, particularly the state. A person is negatively free to the extent that no one is stopping them from doing what they wish. This is the dominant liberal conception: rights as shields, the state's role as one of non-interference.

**Positive liberty** is freedom *to* — the capacity to actually realise one's potential, to be genuinely self-determining, to exercise meaningful agency in one's own life. This requires more than the absence of external constraint. It requires the presence of conditions — psychological, social, material — that make real choice possible.

The distinction is not merely academic. Paulina is, at the moment the play begins, negatively free. She is not imprisoned. She is not being tortured. The state is no longer actively harming her. By the terms of negative liberty, she is free. Yet she cannot sleep when the wind blows. She cannot be alone. She cannot hear a particular voice or piece of music without her body reliving what was done to it. She is, in any meaningful sense, profoundly unfree. The question the play forces is whether a society that provides only negative liberty — the cessation of active harm — can honestly describe itself as free or just.

Positive liberty demands that a just society ask not only "are we stopping ourselves from hurting our citizens?" but "have we created the conditions in which our citizens can actually live freely?" For Paulina, those conditions include acknowledgement of her suffering, accountability for her abuser, and the dismantling of the impunity that allows her tormentor to drive past her house. None of these are provided by the transition. She is negatively free and positively imprisoned.

## A Third Term: Republican Liberty

Philip Pettit's *republicanism* offers a third conception that is particularly useful for reading the play's political context. Republican liberty is freedom from *domination* — not merely from actual interference, but from the *capacity* of another to interfere arbitrarily. What makes tyranny tyrannical is not only that it acts against you, but that it *can* — that you live under a power that might at any moment exert itself over you, and that you have no recourse.

This is Paulina's condition even after the transition. Roberto Miranda is free. He is apparently respectable: a doctor who moves easily in the new democracy and seeks out one of its new officials. Nothing has changed in his capacity to harm her — or others. The structures that protected him, that enabled what he did, have been reformed in name but not dismantled in substance. She lives under the *shadow* of a power that was exercised against her and has never been genuinely held to account. Republican liberty would require not just that Miranda stops torturing people, but that the conditions that made him untouchable are systematically removed.

## Freedom and the Body

A just society's account of freedom must grapple with the body — and this is where the gendered dimension of the lens becomes unavoidable. Liberalism's classic conception of freedom operates with an implicitly disembodied subject: a rational agent who weighs options and makes choices. But freedom is always also a bodily condition. Torture is the state's most extreme assertion of dominion over the individual body — a claim that this body, and its experience, belong to the state's purposes, not to the person who inhabits it.

Paulina's trauma is stored in her body. Her freedom, in any positive sense, requires her body to be returned to her — not just legally, but experientially. This cannot be legislated. It cannot be delivered by a commission. It requires, at minimum, acknowledgement: the formal recognition that what was done to her body was a crime, that she is not fabricating it, that her abuser is responsible. Without this, her body remains, in a psychological sense, still under the jurisdiction of what was done to it.

This is also why the concept of *bodily autonomy* — the right of individuals to sovereignty over their own bodies — is so central to reading the play. It was precisely this autonomy that was violated. A just society's commitment to freedom must include a commitment to the conditions that restore bodily sovereignty to those from whom it has been taken. Impunity forecloses this. It says, in effect: what was done to your body will not be answered for, and you must accommodate yourself to living alongside the person who did it.

## The Paradox of Freedom in Transition

Transitional democracies present a specific form of the liberty problem. They are societies that are, by definition, in the process of *becoming* free — moving from authoritarian control to constitutional governance. The transition is necessarily incomplete. Former perpetrators remain embedded in institutions, in communities, sometimes in power. Survivors live alongside their tormentors. The new order is built on the foundations — personnel, property, social norms — of the old.

In this context, the declaration of freedom is partly performative: the state *asserts* that freedom now exists in order to help bring it into being. But for whom does freedom actually exist in a transitional moment? Those who held power under the old regime retain social capital, impunity, and institutional protection. Those who were victimised by it are asked to accommodate the transition — to not insist too loudly, to accept partial measures, to trust the process. Freedom in transition is therefore distributed unevenly: the perpetrator's freedom is real and present; the survivor's freedom is deferred, conditional, and contingent on her compliance with the state's preferred pace and mechanism of change.

## Essential Questions Through This Lens

**On negative vs. positive liberty:**
- Is the cessation of active harm sufficient to describe a person as free? If not, what further conditions must be met, and who is responsible for creating them?
- If Paulina is technically free but psychologically imprisoned by what was done to her, in what sense has her society delivered on its promise of freedom?
- What would it mean for the state to take positive liberty seriously — to commit not just to not harming citizens, but to actively creating the conditions in which all citizens can live freely?

**On domination and republican liberty:**
- Does Paulina have genuine liberty if Roberto Miranda is free, unpunished, and living nearby? Can she be free whilst he retains the social standing and impunity that enabled his crimes?
- What is the relationship between accountability and freedom? Does holding a perpetrator responsible restore something to the survivor, and if so, what is the name of that thing?
- Is a society in which some citizens can dominate others without consequence — even if they are currently choosing not to — a free society?

**On bodily autonomy and the justice of freedom:**
- What does freedom mean for someone whose body has been made the site of state violence? Is freedom, for such a person, primarily a political category or a psychological and physical one?
- Can a just society claim to guarantee freedom if it does not guarantee the conditions under which survivors of state violence can recover sovereignty over their own experience?
- Why is acknowledgement — the simple act of naming and recognising what was done — so important to freedom? What does its absence cost?

**On the paradox of transitional freedom:**
- In a transitional democracy, who is free first? What does the order of freedom tell us about which lives the new state considers most important?
- When a state asks survivors to accept impunity in exchange for stability, it is asking them to pay for freedom with their own justice. Is this exchange legitimate? Can it ever be?
- If freedom is always unfinished — always in the process of becoming — how do we hold states accountable for the gap between their claims and their citizens' actual lives?

## Vocabulary for This Lens

**Negative liberty** — freedom defined as the absence of external interference or constraint. Associated with classical liberalism and with thinkers such as Locke, Mill, and Berlin. Its limitation is that it measures freedom purely by what is not being done to a person, without regard for whether that person has any real capacity for self-determination.

**Positive liberty** — freedom defined as the genuine capacity for self-determination: the presence of conditions that make meaningful choice and agency possible. Requires attention to psychological, social, and material factors. A person in the grip of severe trauma, however unconstrained by law, does not possess positive liberty.

**Republican liberty (non-domination)** — freedom defined as the absence of another's capacity to interfere arbitrarily in one's life, regardless of whether that capacity is currently being exercised. Associated with Philip Pettit. The important insight is that living *under* the shadow of a power that could act against you — even if it currently chooses not to — is itself a form of unfreedom.

**Bodily autonomy** — the right of persons to sovereignty over their own bodies. Torture is the most extreme violation of this right. Its restoration — which requires more than the cessation of torture — is a condition of genuine freedom.

**The social contract (revisited through liberty)** — the agreement between individuals and the state is not merely about security. It is also a guarantee of freedom. When the state violates bodily autonomy, it has negated the very terms of the contract. A state rebuilding legitimacy after such violation must address not only future freedoms but the freedom it destroyed.

**Transitional freedom** — the uneven, incomplete, and politically managed distribution of freedom in a society moving from authoritarian to democratic governance. Characterised by asymmetry: the freedom of former perpetrators is immediate and structural; the freedom of survivors is deferred, conditional, and dependent on their compliance with the state's preferred mechanisms of transition.

**Impunity as unfreedom** — the condition in which a perpetrator's freedom from consequence actively constrains a survivor's freedom of movement, psychological recovery, and civic participation. In this sense, the perpetrator's freedom and the survivor's freedom are not compatible: granting one forecloses the other.

**Civic freedom** — the freedom to participate fully and equally in one's society: to speak, to be heard, to access institutions, to trust that the state will act on one's behalf. Paulina's psychological condition, produced by state violence and sustained by impunity, effectively excludes her from civic freedom.

**The just society (substantive vs. formal)** — a *formally* just society is one whose laws and institutions are designed to treat all citizens equally. A *substantively* just society is one in which the actual lived conditions of its citizens reflect genuine equality of freedom, dignity, and opportunity. The transition delivers formal justice; Paulina's condition is evidence that formal and substantive justice are not the same.

## Connecting the Lens Back Across the Essential Questions

This liberty framework is not a self-contained addition to the unit; it is a lens that refracts and deepens every question already established.

EQ 1 (justice vs. revenge) is transformed by asking: does pursuing justice restore positive liberty, whilst the denial of justice maintains the domination that makes Paulina unfree?

EQ 3 (truth without confession) becomes: without acknowledgement, can a survivor's sense of reality — and therefore her psychological freedom — be restored?

EQ 5 (whose suffering is recognised) is, at root, a question about whose freedom the state considers worth protecting.

EQ 8 (reconciliation without accountability) is now readable as: can there be freedom — for the survivor — without the conditions that make genuine self-determination possible?

EQ 9 (gender and justice) connects here through the body: the gendered subject of liberty is always embodied, and the freedom of women has historically been measured differently, managed differently, and violated with greater structural impunity.

EQ 10 (justice within an unjust system) becomes the ultimate test of whether formal freedom can be experienced as real freedom by those whose bodies and psychologies still carry the marks of what the system did.
