# Hannah Arendt and Voltaire on Justice, Freedom, and the State
### A Philosophical Intervention for *Death and the Maiden*

---

## HANNAH ARENDT

### Intellectual Context

Arendt (1906–1975) wrote from direct experience of totalitarianism — as a German-Jewish refugee who fled Nazi Germany, spent years stateless, and spent her intellectual life trying to understand how political catastrophe becomes possible and what human freedom requires in order to survive it. Her major works — *The Origins of Totalitarianism* (1951), *The Human Condition* (1958), and *Eichmann in Jerusalem* (1963) — are all, in different ways, attempts to think through the relationship between political organisation, individual agency, and the conditions under which justice is possible.

---

### What Arendt Would Say to Paulina

Arendt would recognise Paulina's condition with immediate and profound clarity. Her concept of **statelessness** describes not just the legal condition of having no state, but the existential condition of being outside the protection of any political community. Paulina is not legally stateless, but she is *politically* stateless in the most important sense: she belongs to a political order that cannot see her, cannot hear her in the register she requires, and cannot deliver what she needs.

Arendt's most enduring insight is that **the right to have rights** — the foundational political entitlement — is not guaranteed by nature or by abstract declarations of human dignity. It is guaranteed only by belonging to a political community that recognises you as a full member. Paulina has, formally, that membership. But the play exposes it as hollow: her testimony does not count in the ways that matter, her knowledge is not legally actionable, and the community is asking her to absorb the cost of its stability.

Arendt would be particularly alert to the **banality of evil** as it manifests in Miranda. Her analysis of Adolf Eichmann argued that he was not a monster but a bureaucrat: a man who had ceased to think, who performed his role without engaging his moral imagination, who used the language of duty and procedure to insulate himself from the human meaning of what he was doing. Miranda, if guilty, is a version of this: a professional who understood his actions as service, as necessity, as the regrettable but functional work of political order.

For Arendt, this has a direct implication for justice. The Eichmann trial mattered to her not primarily as punishment but as **public narrative** — the act of bringing a crime into the shared space of political life, making it visible, named, and collectively acknowledged. This is precisely what Paulina cannot obtain. The Commission is not a trial. Miranda will not be named. A community that cannot bring its crimes into the light of shared public life has not genuinely confronted what was done in its name.

---

### Arendt on Freedom and Political Action

Arendt's conception of freedom is distinctive and important. For her, freedom is **political action** — the capacity to begin something new in the world, to act in concert with others, to insert oneself into public life as a genuine agent.

The precondition for this kind of freedom is the **public realm** — a shared space of appearance where individuals can speak and act and be seen by others as fully human. Totalitarianism destroys the public realm: it atomises citizens, destroys trust, and eliminates the conditions in which genuine political action is possible. What it leaves behind — what Paulina inhabits — is isolation, fear, and the collapse of the shared world that makes freedom meaningful.

Paulina's seizure of Miranda is, from this angle, a desperate attempt to create a public realm of her own — a space in which she can speak, be heard, demand acknowledgement, and act as a genuine political agent. It is improvised, private, and illegal. But it is also the only form of political action available to her. Arendt might read it with profound ambivalence: it is an assertion of freedom through action, but it is a lonely, isolated action — *without* the plurality, the shared world, the institutional recognition, that genuine political freedom requires.

---

### Arendt on Forgiveness and Promising

Arendt is one of the few political philosophers to take forgiveness seriously as a *political* concept rather than merely a personal or religious one. In *The Human Condition* she argues that forgiveness is the human capacity to release others from the irreversibility of past action — to interrupt the chain of consequence that an act sets in motion.

But — and this is crucial — Arendt insists that **forgiveness must be mutual and voluntary**. It cannot be imposed by institutions, mandated by commissions, or extracted as a political necessity. When the state effectively commands survivors to forgive — or to accept impunity as its functional equivalent — it is not facilitating forgiveness. It is eliminating the survivor's agency over the only power she has left: the power to decide whether to release her abuser from the consequences of his actions.

The Commission's amnesty is, in Arendt's terms, a **false forgiveness** — a politically managed simulation of the human act, which bypasses the survivor entirely and delivers its benefits to the perpetrator and the state.

## VOLTAIRE

### Intellectual Context

Voltaire (François-Marie Arouet, 1694–1778) is the Enlightenment's great polemicist — ferociously intelligent, bracingly irreverent, and constitutionally incapable of tolerating the abuse of power by institutions that claim divine or natural authority for their actions. He fought, through journalism and correspondence, against specific, concrete cases of injustice: the Calas affair, the Chevalier de la Barre, the torture and arbitrary imprisonment permitted by the French legal system. His philosophy is inseparable from his activism.

---

### What Voltaire Would Say About the State and the Individual

Voltaire's central preoccupation was **arbitrary power** — the exercise of authority without accountability, without transparency, and without rational justification. He would recognise the authoritarian regime that tortured Paulina immediately, and he would not reach for nuanced analysis. He would be incandescent.

His response to the Commission would be equally sharp. He would have no patience for the argument that impunity is a necessary price of stability. He would ask: *necessary for whom? Stable for whom?* He understood that appeals to the collective good are habitually made by those who benefit from the arrangement being protected.

---

### Voltaire on Torture

His essay *On Torture* (from the *Philosophical Dictionary*) is a masterpiece of controlled outrage. He argues that torture as a legal instrument is not only morally abominable but rationally incoherent: it produces unreliable information, it punishes before conviction, and it operates on the premise that the state has the right to treat human bodies as instruments of its own purposes.

He would read *Death and the Maiden* as a play about what happens when a society that tortured its citizens attempts to call itself civilised without first dismantling the structures that made torture possible. The society in the play has changed its name. It has written a new constitution. It has appointed a Commission. What it has not done is held to account the people who operated the torture chambers. Voltaire would note that this is not a transition to civilisation. It is civilisation as performance, conducted over a foundation of preserved barbarism.

His particular fury would be directed at the **instrumentalisation of human beings** — the use of persons as means rather than ends. Paulina was not a person to her torturers. She was a problem to be managed, a source of information, an obstacle to be broken. A society which permits this and then declines to prosecute it has not repudiated the logic of instrumentalisation. It has merely suspended its application — for now.

---

### Voltaire on Freedom and Reason

Voltaire believed that human beings have the capacity for reason and that this capacity is the basis of both freedom and dignity. A just society is one organised so as to allow that capacity to develop and function: free from arbitrary imprisonment, free from torture, free from the suppression of thought and speech.

He would read the situation in *Death and the Maiden* as a society that has not yet earned the right to call itself free, because it has not yet subjected its own recent past to honest rational scrutiny. "Tout est pour le mieux dans le meilleur des mondes possibles" — everything is for the best in the best of all possible worlds — is *Candide*'s satirical target: the intellectual refusal to look clearly at what is actually happening. The transition narrative performs this same refusal.

---

### Voltaire on the Victim's Right to Anger

Voltaire did not counsel patience, procedural compliance, or the subordination of personal moral conviction to institutional process. He would not tell Paulina to trust the Commission. He would recognise in her anger not madness or irrationality but the entirely appropriate response of a rational being to a situation in which reason and justice have been suspended on her behalf without her consent.

He would, however, press her on method. Voltaire believed in the power of the public word — in making injustice visible, in naming and shaming. He would want Paulina's truth in the open. He would want Miranda named. He would want the Commission's limitations exposed. The weapon he would give her is not a gun but a voice — amplified, relentless, and perfectly aimed.

---

## Convergence and Divergence: The Productive Tension

They agree on the essentials: the state has no right to dispose of individuals as instruments of its purposes; impunity is not a legitimate political arrangement; and a society that does not honestly confront what has been done in its name is not genuinely free.

But their emphases diverge in ways that matter for reading the play.

**Arendt** is most concerned with the *political conditions of freedom* — the shared public realm, plurality, the capacity for action-in-concert. Her Paulina is a figure who has been excluded from the public realm and is attempting, desperately and in isolation, to reconstruct one. Arendt would mourn the loneliness of what Paulina is doing as much as the injustice that forced her to do it. She would ask: what kind of political community failed to create the conditions in which Paulina's claim could be heard publicly, collectively, without her having to hold a man at gunpoint in her own living room?

**Voltaire** is most concerned with the *abuse of power by institutions* and the individual's right — even duty — to resist it through reason, speech, and public exposure. His Paulina is a figure whose anger is rational and whose cause is just, but who has been denied the public instruments through which rational anger can be made effective.

Arendt emphasises the **systemic and structural**: what has been destroyed is the shared world, the conditions of plurality, the public realm. Voltaire emphasises the **particular and immediate**: this man, this crime, this impunity.

Together they offer the play's central irresolution from two directions: Arendt asks what kind of society must be built so that what happened to Paulina becomes impossible; Voltaire asks what must be done right now, in this room, about this man. The play, characteristically, refuses to let either answer be sufficient.

---

## Guiding Questions Through These Thinkers

**Through Arendt:**
- Has the transition created a genuine public realm — a space in which Paulina's experience can be spoken, heard, and collectively acknowledged — or merely a formal political structure that leaves her outside it?
- Is Miranda's potential impunity an example of the banality of evil: evil that persists not through monstrous will but through institutional protection and the failure to think?
- What would Arendt mean by saying that Paulina's act in the play is a form of political action? What does it achieve, and what does its isolation cost?

**Through Voltaire:**
- What would Voltaire's response be to the argument that prosecuting torturers would destabilise the transition? Would he find it persuasive, and why or why not?
- Voltaire fought his campaigns through public exposure. What does it mean that Paulina has no public platform — that her only available space of confrontation is her own living room?
- Is Gerardo's faith in the Commission a form of the comfortable narrative Voltaire spent his life attacking?

**Convergence question:**
- Both Arendt and Voltaire would insist that what matters is not just what a society claims to be, but what it actually does to the most vulnerable of its members. By that measure, how free and just is the society in *Death and the Maiden* at the moment the play begins?
