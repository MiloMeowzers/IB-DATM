# Dorfman's Playscript Through Brecht
### An Epic Theatre Reading of *Death and the Maiden*

---

## Framing the Encounter

The relationship between Dorfman and Brecht is not one of simple discipleship. Dorfman does not write Epic Theatre in the orthodox sense — *Death and the Maiden* lacks the songs, the placards, the overtly didactic machinery that characterises canonical Brecht. Yet reading the playscript closely through Brechtian theory reveals something important: Dorfman has internalised many of Brecht's central concerns and methods and has adapted them to a post-authoritarian political context that Brecht himself never fully anticipated.

The productive approach is not to ask "is *Death and the Maiden* a Brechtian play?" but rather "what does Brechtian theory illuminate about how this playscript works, and where does the play resist or complicate what Brecht would have it do?"

---

## BRECHT: THE CORE THEORETICAL ARCHITECTURE

### The Problem Brecht Was Solving

Brecht's theoretical project — developed across the essays collected as *Brecht on Theatre*, most importantly "The Modern Theatre is the Epic Theatre" (1930) and "A Short Organum for the Theatre" (1948) — was a response to what he regarded as the political bankruptcy of Aristotelian dramatic theatre. He argued that conventional theatre, organised around emotional identification, catharsis, and the illusionist representation of reality, was fundamentally conservative: it encouraged audiences to *feel* rather than *think*, to experience vicarious resolution rather than recognise the social contradictions staged before them.

The theory of the **Verfremdungseffekt** (V-effect, or alienation/estrangement effect) describes the theatrical strategy of preventing the audience from losing itself in the fiction — of constantly reminding spectators that they are watching a constructed representation of social reality, not reality itself, and that the conditions being staged are historical, contingent, and therefore changeable.

Brecht's **Gestus** — the physical-social attitude embodied in a character at any given moment — was the actor's primary tool. **Epic structure** replaced causal dramatic development with episodes that could be rearranged, that carry their own meaning without requiring the context of what precedes or follows, and that present social contradictions without resolving them into cathartic closure.

## THE PLAY'S STRUCTURE: EPIC OR ARISTOTELIAN?

### Surface Appearances

At first glance, *Death and the Maiden* appears stubbornly Aristotelian. It observes the classical unities — single location (the beach house), compressed time (one night and the following morning), continuous causally connected action. It has three characters, escalating tension, a moment of crisis, and something resembling a denouement. It seems designed to produce exactly what Brecht wanted to prevent: emotional immersion, suspense, identification.

This reading is superficial. A closer examination of the playscript reveals that Dorfman has embedded Brechtian estrangement mechanisms into what appears to be a conventional well-made play.

---

### The Structural V-Effect: Irresolution as Estrangement

Brecht's most fundamental estrangement device at the structural level was the refusal of cathartic resolution. *Death and the Maiden* enacts this refusal with extraordinary precision. The play's ending — Paulina and Gerardo at a concert, Miranda (real, or an illusion in Paulina’s head) taking another seat, his eyes briefly meeting hers, the lights going down while the music plays — does not tell us whether Miranda is guilty. It does not tell us whether Paulina has achieved justice, revenge, or neither. It withholds every resolution the audience's investment in the narrative has been building towards.

This is not a failure of dramatic nerve. It is a structural V-effect: the moment at which the audience is prevented from the emotional discharge that would allow them to leave the theatre having *felt* the problem rather than *thought* it. You cannot leave having experienced justice vicariously through Paulina, because the play refuses to confirm that justice occurred. You are sent into the world still holding the question, which is precisely where Brecht wanted his audiences.

Dorfman's deliberate choice not to resolve the question of Miranda's guilt is also politically precise: in the post-Pinochet Chile that provided the play's context, the question of perpetrators' guilt was also officially unresolved, officially unverifiable, officially managed into ambiguity by exactly the kind of Commission the play stages. The audience's frustration at not knowing enacts their implication in a world that has decided not to know.

---

### The Three-Act Structure as Gestus

**Act One** establishes the social order and then breaks it. Gerardo arrives, tells Paulina about the Commission and the stranger who helped him on the road; Miranda arrives late that night and drinks with Gerardo while Paulina listens unseen. In a wordless scene she ties him to a chair, and by dawn she has announced her trial. The act's opening Gestus is the Gestus of *managed transition* — the careful, liberal-democratic handling of a dangerous past. Gerardo performs competence, care, and procedural authority. Paulina performs domesticity and gratitude. Miranda performs helpfulness and ordinary decency. This is, in Brechtian terms, the ideological surface.

**Act Two** extends the rupture that closes Act One: with Miranda bound and gagged, Paulina conducts her own trial, recording it on the cassette recorder. The act's Gestus is the Gestus of *dispossessed authority* — a woman who has no standing in the official process asserting standing by force. We can now see, because they have been disrupted, exactly how the power arrangements of the transition are distributed.

Act Two also stages the interrogation not just of Miranda but of *Gerardo* — and by extension, of the liberal, procedural position the audience may be occupying. Gerardo's arguments are rational and legally correct, and the play makes them feel inadequate. The Gestus of his position — the well-meaning man of process who cannot see what his process has cost his wife — is exposed as a social attitude with political consequences.

**Act Three** is the play's most Brechtian movement. Miranda's "confession" — which may be genuine, coerced, fabricated, or strategically calculated — is followed by his retraction, Paulina's claim that he corrected the errors she planted, her countdown, and the descending mirror; then comes the coda: the concert hall, the ambiguity, the image that refuses interpretation. The act's Gestus is the Gestus of *unresolved coexistence*. The question the coda poses is not dramatic but political: *what are the conditions under which such coexistence is possible, and are those conditions just?*

## THE V-EFFECT AT THE LOCAL LEVEL: SPECIFIC DISTANCIATION DEVICES

### The Schubert

The play's title and its recurring structural element — the Schubert String Quartet in D minor, *Death and the Maiden* — is a Brechtian device of considerable sophistication. Music in Brecht's theatre does not illustrate the dramatic action; it *comments* on it, creates distance from it, introduces a third perspective not available to any character within the drama.

Schubert's quartet is both beautiful and about death. It sets a death-haunted Romantic melody against an inexorable accompanying pulse — the formal relationship between the two voices enacting exactly the play's central tension: beauty and horror in irresolvable coexistence. Paulina's abusers played it during her torture. It is now simultaneously the music she loves and the instrument of her violation.

Each time the music is deployed in the playscript it functions as an estrangement device: it breaks the dramatic illusion just enough to remind the audience that they are in the presence of constructed meaning, that Dorfman has made choices. The audience hears what the characters cannot fully hear — the formal structure of what has been done to Paulina, and what she is trying to reclaim.

---

### The Stranger in the Dark

Act One's inciting incident — Gerardo bringing a stranger into the house — is presented with deliberate narrative opacity. The audience sees and hears the stranger as Paulina does, while she listens unseen; only in the next scene does it learn that she has recognised his voice. Dorfman gives no authorial reassurance.

This is a V-effect of a specifically political kind. The audience is prevented from the comfortable position of the omniscient spectator. They cannot know whether Miranda is guilty. They are placed in the position that transitional societies occupy in relation to their own history: confronting a past that cannot be verified, in which the only available testimony is the survivor's body and memory, and in which the institutional mechanisms for establishing truth are structurally compromised.

---

### Paulina's Monologue as Lehrstück

Paulina gives her account of what was done to her twice: first to the bound Miranda over breakfast in Act One, Scene 4, as the Schubert plays, and then in full, recorded by Gerardo as if before the Commission, in Act Three, Scene 1. In both, she speaks to one listener while the audience overhears. This speech is the play's most Brechtian moment. It is testimony in the classical sense: direct address, first-person authority, the claim of experience. But it is also structured as a *Lehrstück* — a teaching play within the play — in which the audience is taught, through the specificity of Paulina's account, exactly what political torture is, how it operates, and what it does to a person.

The content of the speech performs what Brecht called *historicisation*: it locates what was done to Paulina in a specific political moment, with specific institutional arrangements, by specific kinds of people operating under specific social conditions. It refuses the abstraction that would allow the audience to experience her suffering aesthetically rather than politically.

Neither account is addressed to the audience — but the audience is present at both. Miranda is the specific perpetrator; the audience is the society that permitted him. The double address is a Brechtian theatrical trick: the audience is never allowed to be merely observers.

---

### Gerardo as the Audience's Representative

One of Brecht's most sophisticated structural devices was the use of a character who represents the audience's own ideological position within the drama — a figure whose responses model how the bourgeois, liberal, well-meaning spectator tends to think, and whose inadequacy exposes the limits of that thinking.

Gerardo is this figure in *Death and the Maiden*. He is educated, decent, professionally committed, politically engaged, and entirely confident in the framework of liberal proceduralism. These are the beliefs of the play's likely audience. Dorfman stages those beliefs being tested, and found insufficient. Not wrong — Gerardo is not a villain or a fool — but insufficient. Watching Gerardo is watching the audience's own position dramatised and found wanting, which is the V-effect at its most precise: recognition followed by discomfort followed by the necessity of thought.

## WHERE DORFMAN DEPARTS FROM BRECHT

### The Body and Emotion as Political Instruments

Brecht's hostility to emotional identification was tactical rather than absolute. But his theory consistently positions feeling as a danger to critical thought: the emotionally immersed spectator is the politically passive spectator.

*Death and the Maiden* takes a different position. Dorfman insists on emotional engagement with Paulina not as a distraction from political thought but as its *precondition*. The audience must feel what Paulina's situation is before they can think about the political structures that produced it. The emotional impact of her testimony is not an obstacle to political analysis but its most powerful instrument.

This is a reflection of the specific political context. Brecht was writing about class structures and fascism in ways that his middle-class audience needed to be prevented from experiencing safely at a comfortable emotional distance. Dorfman is writing about torture, trauma, and gender-based state violence in a context where the audience's problem is not emotional over-identification but *emotional distance* — the comfortable abstraction that turns atrocity into political category rather than bodily reality. For this audience, feeling *is* thinking.

---

### The Impossibility of the Gestus for Trauma

Brecht's Gestus presupposes that social attitudes can be physically embodied, read, and critiqued. This works well for characters whose social role is legible, stable, and consistent.

Paulina presents a problem for the Gestus. Her body does not express a stable social attitude. It expresses the *disruption* of stable social attitude — the traumatised body that does not behave according to social script, that acts from a logic the social world cannot read as rational. The Gestus cannot capture this because trauma exceeds the social.

Dorfman's great formal achievement is staging a body that exceeds Brechtian categorisation. This is not a failure of Brechtian method but a limit of it, and *Death and the Maiden* marks that limit precisely.

---

## THE AUDIENCE AS THE COMMISSION

The play's most audacious Brechtian move operates at the level of the theatrical event itself rather than at the level of the playscript.

The play cannot tell us whether Miranda is guilty. It does not have the evidence. Neither does the audience. We sit in the dark, watching Paulina's testimony, watching Miranda's denials, watching Gerardo's attempts at procedural management, and we cannot know. We are in exactly the position of the transitional justice mechanism that the play critiques: presented with testimony that cannot be corroborated, a perpetrator who claims innocence with apparent sincerity, a survivor whose evidence is her own experience and her body's knowledge, and no institutional mechanism capable of producing the truth.

What the audience does with that uncertainty is not incidental to the play's meaning. It *is* the play's meaning. The theatre has made us the Commission. And by staging the Commission's impossibility from the inside, it forces us to confront what we would do, what we could do, and what kind of society we would need in order to do better.

This is Brechtian theatre at its most complete: not a play about a political problem, but a theatrical event that enacts the political problem in the relationship between the performance and the audience, and sends the audience into the world not with answers but with the full weight of the question.

## Summary: Dorfman and Brecht — A Comparative Taxonomy

| Brechtian Device | Brecht's Use | Dorfman's Adaptation |
|---|---|---|
| V-effect / estrangement | Songs, placards, direct address, interrupted narrative | Structural irresolution, epistemic uncertainty, double address |
| Gestus | Physical embodiment of stable social role | Traumatised body that exceeds social categorisation |
| Epic structure | Episodic, non-linear, rearrangeable | Three-act but causally disrupted; Act Three refuses resolution |
| Music | Commentary that distances and critiques | Schubert as fused beauty/atrocity; musical memory as political instrument |
| The representative character | Bourgeois figure whose ideology is exposed | Gerardo as liberal proceduralist whose adequacy is tested |
| Parable form | Historical/geographical distance | Geographical namelessness; universality in tension with bodily specificity |
| Audience implication | Critical spectator who recognises social conditions | Audience as Commission: epistemological complicity, irresolvable uncertainty |
| Emotional engagement | Controlled, secondary to critical thought | Central to political argument; feeling as precondition of thinking |

## Essential Questions Through This Lens

- Brecht wanted audiences to leave his theatre asking "how do we change this?" rather than "wasn't that moving?" What question does *Death and the Maiden* want you to leave with — and is it a Brechtian question?
- If Gerardo represents the audience's own ideological position, what does the play's treatment of him ask the audience to recognise about themselves?
- The play refuses to tell us whether Miranda is guilty. Is this Brechtian estrangement (productive discomfort that demands critical thought) or something else — and what is at stake in the distinction?
- Brecht's Gestus assumes that social attitudes can be physically read. What happens to this assumption when the body in question has been destroyed as a social instrument by torture?
- The audience is positioned as the Commission. If so, what verdict do you deliver — and what does the fact that reasonable people deliver different verdicts tell us about the play's political argument?

## Additional Vocabulary

**Verfremdungseffekt (V-effect)** — the theatrical strategy of preventing emotional absorption and maintaining critical distance in the audience, through devices that remind spectators that they are watching a constructed representation of social reality.

**Gestus** — the physical-social attitude embodied by a character at a given moment: the way social relations, power structures, and ideological positions are encoded in bodily comportment and staged behaviour.

**Epic Theatre** — Brecht's alternative to Aristotelian dramatic theatre: episodic, non-linear, didactic, focused on social conditions rather than individual psychology, designed to produce critical thought rather than cathartic emotion.

**Historicisation** — the Brechtian technique of locating human behaviour in specific historical and social conditions rather than presenting it as universal or natural; making the contingent visible as contingent.

**Lehrstück** — teaching play; a Brechtian form in which the theatrical event is explicitly organised around instruction, where the purpose is the transmission of understanding rather than the production of aesthetic experience.

**Catharsis (Aristotelian)** — the emotional purgation achieved by tragic drama, through which the audience is discharged of pity and fear. Brecht's explicit target: catharsis allows the audience to feel the problem resolved without thinking it changed.

**The fourth wall** — the imaginary barrier between performer and audience, the maintenance of which is central to the illusionist theatre Brecht opposed.

**Theatrical complicity** — the audience's implication in the meaning of the theatrical event: not merely as observers but as participants whose responses, judgements, and interpretive choices are part of what the play produces.
