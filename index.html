How to use this guide

Every event, claim and quotation in this guide is tied to the playscript. References take the form II.1.95: Act II, Scene 1, line 95, where each line of dialogue counts as one line, counted from the start of the scene. A reference with a plus sign, such as II.1.95+, points to the text that follows that line: a stage direction, or the continuation of a long line of dialogue. Act I, Scene 3 has no dialogue at all.

Built for Paper 2. The issue arcs trace each concern through Acts I, II and III, so you can see how the play’s position develops. The pairing notes at the end of the exam section show how the play sets up against The Visit and The Things They Carried.

The examination

Course and paperIB Diploma English A: Language and Literature, Paper 2: the comparative essay on two literary works studied in the course. Paper 2 is not a set-text exam: the questions are general, they do not name any work, and there are no extracts.
Format1 hour 45 minutes at both SL and HL. Answer one of four general questions, comparing two of the literary works you have studied. 25 marks.
CriteriaA Knowledge, understanding and interpretation (5). B Analysis and evaluation (10), in two strands: B1 how meaning is shaped by language, technique, style and broader authorial choices (5); B2 the similarities and differences between the two works (5). C Focus and organisation (5). D Language (5).
What this means for revisionYou will not know the question in advance, so you cannot prepare a single essay. You prepare a small number of moments from each work that you know in detail, grouped by issue, and you rehearse how each work handles those issues differently. That is what the issue arcs, the event bank and the quote bank in this guide are for. Comparison must be structural: every paragraph needs both works, and criterion B2 assesses it directly.
iSWIM for Paper 2. Use the five guiding questions on every prompt.
iWhat is the issue of significance? The concern beneath the prompt’s topic word.
SWhy does it matter? The human, social or philosophical stakes.
WWhat does each author want you to understand about it?
IHow does each author get you to understand it? Structure, form, characterisation, voice, staging.
MWhat does each work position its audience towards, and what does the comparison reveal? The essay-level M comes from the tension between M(A) and M(B).

Write each paragraph with PEACE ACT: Point, Evidence, Analysis, Comparison, Extension, Audience, Context, Tie-back. Characters do not exist: reach through Paulina, Gerardo and Roberto to the choices Dorfman makes with them.

Context

Facts about the writer and period are attributed to a named source. Facts about the work come from the work.

Where and when

The play names no country. The characters refer to seventeen years of dictatorship I.1.82 and to Paulina’s ordeal fifteen years earlier II.1.41, and she dates the arrest to 6 April 1975 III.1.56. The new government’s Investigating Commission and the threat from the Army I.2.39 place the action in a transition from military rule. It is usually read as Chile after Pinochet.

Source: Playscript

Theatrical lenses

The play can be read through Brecht (the mirror and the refusal of catharsis), Arendt (the banality of the functionary), Kafka (the process without a verdict) and Voltaire. These are interpretive frames to test against the play, not facts about Dorfman’s intentions; see Dig deeper.

Event bank

16 numbered events in order. Read one scene for close revision, or E1 to E16 straight through as the arc of the whole work.

Scene I.1

E1 Gerardo comes home late I.1.2–89

Paulina, alone with a gun, waits for Gerardo I.1, opening+. He explains a flat tyre and a stranger who gave him a lift I.1.6. The real subject is the president’s Investigating Commission: Paulina points out that it only investigates cases that ended in death I.1.64.

Why it matters. The play opens on the limits of the new state’s justice before the doctor arrives. The gun is on stage in the first minute.

Scene I.2

E2 Roberto arrives I.2.2–58

The stranger, Dr Roberto Miranda, returns late at night, having heard Gerardo’s name on the news I.2.11. Over drinks the men discuss whether the Commission can work I.2.39, and Roberto quotes Nietzsche on the female soul I.2.22. Gerardo invites him to stay the night.

Why it matters. The audience hears the details Paulina will later claim to recognise before it knows they matter.

E3 Roberto’s outrage I.2.30–36

Over drinks Roberto regrets that the amnesty protects the old regime’s men I.2.30 and declares he would kill all of them I.2.36.

Why it matters. The future accused is first heard as the loudest voice for punishment.

Dig deeper. It could be read as performed outrage, the camouflage of a perpetrator blending into the new order. Is it evidence of guilt, or only evidence that the play has made you suspicious?

Scene I.3

E4 The wordless scene I.3, opening+

Paulina takes the gun and goes into Roberto’s room; there is a muffled cry I.3, opening+. When his own headlights sweep the room, Roberto is revealed tied to a chair and gagged I.3, opening+.

Why it matters. Dorfman stages the turning point without a word, and shows the result only as an image in a flash of light.

Scene I.4

E5 The accusation I.4.1–54

Paulina plays Roberto’s cassette of Schubert’s Death and the Maiden I.4.1+. She tells Gerardo that this is the doctor who played Schubert while she was tortured I.4.13 and announces a trial in the house I.4.54.

Why it matters. The private trial begins as a parody of the courts the new state cannot use.

Dig deeper. Gerardo’s response is to call Paulina sick I.4.20. Notice who is diagnosed and who is believed at each point of the play.

Scene II.1

E6 The trial and the terms II.1.7–95

Paulina records everything on the cassette recorder II.1.7. Gerardo threatens that she will have to kill him first II.1.39 and warns how the press will use the episode against the Commission II.1.67. Paulina names what she wants: a confession II.1.95.

Why it matters. The private conflict is framed in the public stakes of the transition.

Dig deeper. The cassette recorder gives a private act the look of official procedure. Is Paulina imitating the Commission, or the interrogation room?

E7 Recognition and the fantasies of revenge II.1.74–95

Paulina tells Gerardo she recognises Roberto’s skin and smell as well as his voice II.1.74+. She admits that for years she imagined revenge on men like him II.1.89, including having him raped II.1.91, and that she put these fantasies aside: what she wants is a confession II.1.95.

Why it matters. Dorfman shows the revenge fantasy and its rejection in the same speech. Paulina chooses speech over violence before the audience sees her threaten to kill.

Dig deeper. Her evidence is bodily: voice, skin, smell. What kind of proof is that, and why can no institution in the play hear it?

E8 The dead and the living II.1.68–104

Paulina argues that the Commission deals only with the dead, and she can speak II.1.68. Gerardo tells her she has stayed a prisoner for fifteen years II.1.79. She sends him to persuade Roberto II.1.104.

Why it matters. The play sets the institution against the survivor’s need to be heard.

Scene II.2

E9 Gerardo and Roberto II.2.21–39

Alone with Gerardo, Roberto pleads innocence and offers innocent reasons for his Schubert cassette II.2.21. Gerardo proposes indulging her II.2.39: a confession, invented if necessary.

Why it matters. The lawyer coaches a confession, so the play makes its own evidence doubtful.

Dig deeper. Gerardo advises Roberto to invent a confession II.2.30. From this point no account the audience hears is free of his influence.

E10 Gerardo’s outburst II.2.50–51

Roberto suggests that the couple are playing good cop and bad cop, and that a real man would kill a wife’s rapist II.2.50. Gerardo explodes in mockery of that idea of manhood II.2.51.

Why it matters. The lawyer’s anger is turned against the code of violent masculinity, while he goes on defending the man accused of embodying it.

Scene III.1

E11 The old wound III.1.8–17

Paulina and Gerardo return to the night she came home after her release and a woman came out of his bedroom III.1.9. Gerardo’s defence is that he was looking for her and is also human III.1.14.

Why it matters. The man who speaks for law and reconciliation is shown asking forgiveness for his own betrayal.

E12 Paulina’s testimony III.1.51–56

Gerardo records Paulina telling her story as if in front of the Commission III.1.52, beginning with her name and the date of her arrest, 6 April 1975 III.1.56.

Why it matters. The private trial becomes a rehearsal of the testimony the state will not hear from her.

E13 The confession III.1.57–65

Roberto’s confession is heard in the dark, describing the Schubert as a way of gaining prisoners’ trust III.1.57+. He writes it out and signs it III.1.65+.

Why it matters. The confession is the play’s most detailed account of the torture, and it may have been written by others.

Dig deeper. The confession is clinical: torture described as a medical procedure that keeps prisoners alive III.1.57+, then as curiosity III.1.58. Who wrote these words, and does it matter if they are true anyway?

E14 The test III.1.84–106

Roberto says the confession is false and was built from what Gerardo told him III.1.90. Paulina reveals that she planted small variations in her story to Gerardo, and Roberto corrected most of them III.1.99. She will kill him not for guilt but because he has not repented III.1.99, and counts towards ten.

Why it matters. The play hands the audience a test of guilt and then refuses to let the verdict settle.

Dig deeper. The audience never sees the correction happen. It hears Paulina’s claim III.1.95+ and Roberto’s denial III.1.94. Which do you believe, and why?

E15 The mirror III.1.107

Paulina asks why people like her must always be the ones to make concessions III.1.107. The actors freeze and a giant mirror descends, forcing the audience to look at themselves III.1.107+.

Why it matters. The question of what she does is handed to the audience; we do not see the outcome.

Dig deeper. The scene freezes on a question III.1.107. The audience is shown itself, not the answer.

Scene III.2

E16 The concert III.2.1

Months later Gerardo talks to admirers about the Commission’s final report III.2.1. Roberto appears, perhaps real, perhaps an illusion III.2.1+. The quartet plays; Paulina and Roberto look at each other, and she faces the stage and the mirror III.2.1+.

Why it matters. Reconciliation is staged as a society that sits in the same room as its torturers and listens to the same music.

Dig deeper. Gerardo praises people for acting without any personal vendetta III.2.1+. Who is that line for?

Characters

Paulina Salas

A survivor of detention and torture under the dictatorship, married to Gerardo; arrested on 6 April 1975 III.1.56.

  • Identifies Roberto by music, voice and phrases rather than sight I.4.13.
  • Demands speech, not death II.1.95, and claims the right to be heard II.1.68.
  • Sets a trap that seems to prove Roberto’s guilt III.1.99.
  • Refuses the role of the one who always concedes III.1.107.

Arc. From hiding with a gun (I.1) to judge (II–III) to a silent figure in the concert hall (III.2).

Gerardo Escobar

A lawyer newly appointed to the president’s Investigating Commission I.2.11.

  • Speaks for due process and moderation, and fears for the Commission II.1.67.
  • Calls Paulina mad in private II.2.39 and helps produce the confession III.1.90.
  • Asks forgiveness for his affair III.1.14.

Arc. Ends successful and public III.2.1: the transition’s face.

Dr Roberto Miranda

The doctor who helps Gerardo on the road and whom Paulina accuses.

  • Arrives because of the Commission I.2.11.
  • Explains his cassette innocently II.2.21.
  • Confesses, then retracts III.1.86, and argues against the cycle of revenge III.1.104.

Arc. The play never decides him; his final appearance may be an illusion III.2.1+.

Themes

Each theme is traced through the work. Quotation numbers refer to the quote bank.

Justice, due process and revenge

The play sets a private trial against public justice that cannot reach the living. Dorfman never lets the audience settle on which is just.

Act IThe Commission investigates only deaths I.1.64. Paulina announces her own trial I.4.54.
Quotations: Q2, Q5, Q6, Q8
Act IIThe trial acquires procedure and terms: recording II.1.7, a defender, and a demand for confession rather than death II.1.95.
Quotations: Q9, Q10, Q11, Q16, Q19
Act IIIThe charge moves from guilt to repentance III.1.99, and Roberto names the cycle of revenge III.1.104. The verdict is never shown.
Quotations: Q28, Q29, Q30, Q32, Q33, Q36

The whole arc. From a trial the state will not hold, to a trial with no possible verdict.

Dig deeper
  • What is the difference between justice and revenge, and at what point in the play does Paulina’s trial cross from one to the other, if it does?
  • Who has the right to judge? Paulina sets herself up as judge I.4.54; Gerardo appeals to due process II.1.6. Which claim does the play make stronger?
  • Is certainty necessary for justice? The play gives Paulina a test that seems to prove guilt and then lets Roberto deny it.

Truth, memory and evidence

Paulina’s evidence is sensory and remembered; Roberto’s confession is coerced and possibly scripted. The play offers proof and withholds certainty.

Act IThe audience hears Roberto’s Nietzsche before it means anything I.2.22; Paulina identifies him by music and voice I.4.13.
Quotations: Q4, Q5, Q7
Act IIPaulina claims the right to speak II.1.68; Roberto offers innocent explanations II.2.21.
Quotations: Q9, Q12, Q13, Q14, Q15, Q16, Q17, Q18
Act IIIPaulina testifies III.1.52; Roberto confesses, then says the confession was written for him III.1.90; her test seems to prove him guilty III.1.99; the last stage direction leaves him real or imagined III.2.1+.
Quotations: Q23, Q24, Q25, Q26, Q27, Q28, Q35

The whole arc. Each act adds evidence and each act weakens it, so the audience ends where Paulina began: certain without proof, or doubtful without reason.

Dig deeper
  • Can the body know something the mind cannot prove? Paulina recognises voice, skin and smell II.1.74+.
  • Can a coerced confession be true, and can a free denial be a lie? The play refuses to let you know.
  • What does your wish to know whether Roberto is guilty reveal about your own need for a resolved story?

Transition, the state and reconciliation

The Escobars’ house is a small version of the country after dictatorship: the victim, the lawyer of the new order and the alleged agent of the old regime under one roof.

Act IThe new government’s Commission is fragile I.2.39.
Quotations: Q2, Q3, Q6
Act IIGerardo fears the episode will destroy the Commission II.1.67.
Quotations: Q11, Q15
Act IIIThe marriage repeats the national formula III.1.17; the final scene shows the report delivered III.2.1 while victim and torturer share a concert hall.
Quotations: Q22, Q23, Q30, Q31, Q33, Q34, Q36

The whole arc. Reconciliation is shown working, and shown costing the person least able to bear it.

Dig deeper
  • Who benefits when a nation chooses to move forward rather than confront what was done?
  • Can reconciliation exist without accountability? Compare Paulina’s demand for a confession with the Commission’s final report III.2.1.
  • When the state and the individual want different things, whose needs come first, and can a state that overrides the individual still claim to guarantee freedom?

Gender and power

The play stages a woman’s testimony judged by two men, one who loves her and one she says raped her. Who is called mad, and who repairs the damage, are recurring questions.

Act IThe first quarrel is about who fixes things I.1.10; Roberto’s Nietzsche concerns the female soul I.2.22.
Quotations: Q1, Q4
Act IIGerardo protects Roberto with his body II.1.39 and calls Paulina mad in private II.2.39.
Quotations: Q10, Q14, Q19
Act IIIPaulina asks why people like her must always concede III.1.107.
Quotations: Q20, Q32

The whole arc. Paulina holds the gun throughout and is still the one asked to give way.

Dig deeper
  • How does gender shape who is believed? Track every moment Paulina is called sick, mad or ill I.4.20 II.2.39.
  • Roberto offers a code of manhood in which a husband kills his wife’s rapist II.2.50; Gerardo mocks it. What does the play suggest about men speaking for women’s injuries?

Complicity and concealment

Gerardo is the play’s voice of law and openness, and he deceives: in the past, by his affair, and in the present, by coaching the confession.

  • If institutions of transitional justice are run by people with their own secrets, what does that say about the institutions?

Dorfman’s methods: the playscript

AO3: how the writer creates meanings and effects through language, structure and form.

Unity of place and time

One house, one night and a day: the classical unities compress a nation’s transition into a room I.1, opening+.

Three characters and shifting alliances

Each scene changes who is aligned with whom: the men drinking together I.2.22, Paulina and Gerardo against each other II.1.39, the two men alone II.2.39.

The wordless scene

Act I, Scene 3 has no dialogue; the turning point is shown as an image in car headlights I.3, opening+.

Sound: music, sea and the cassette recorder

Schubert played in the house I.4.1+; the recorder as courtroom procedure II.1.7; the confession heard in darkness III.1.57+.

The mirror and the audience

A giant mirror makes the audience look at itself at the moment of decision III.1.107+, and faces the actors in the last scene III.2.1+. A Brechtian reading of this device is set out in Dig deeper.

The play as a national microcosm

On the surface. Three people in a beach house over one night and a day.

It could be argued that the house is the nation in small: the living room becomes a courtroom I.4.54, an interrogation room II.1.7 and a negotiating table II.1.104. The personal quarrel is a political one.

Each character carries a public position. Paulina is the survivor whose case the Commission will not hear II.1.68. Gerardo is the new order’s lawyer, anxious about how the press will use the episode II.1.67. Roberto is the professional who may have served the old order and now speaks the language of the new I.2.36.

The marriage repeats the national argument. Gerardo asks Paulina to let go of the past II.1.80, and she hears in it the demand to forget II.1.79+. When the affair returns, he asks for forgiveness in the same words III.1.16.

Questions to explore
  • If the house is the nation, what is the gun?
  • Can a society heal without retribution? Can justice exist without certainty?

Sound: Schubert, the tape and the concert hall

On the surface. The play is named after a piece of music, and the music recurs.

Schubert’s quartet acts almost as a fourth character. Paulina plays it in the house I.4.1+; the confession explains it as a way of winning prisoners’ trust III.1.57+; the play ends with it in public III.2.1+.

The cassette recorder formalises the trial II.1.7 and then makes truth a matter of transcription: Roberto copies his recorded words in his own hand III.1.58+. In the dark, his recorded voice overlaps with Paulina’s and with the music III.1.57+, so the audience cannot separate testimony from confession.

Paulina keeps the confession as insurance: if harm comes to her or Gerardo, it goes to the newspapers II.1.98. Evidence becomes a weapon rather than a step towards a verdict.

Questions to explore
  • Why does Dorfman put Mozart’s Dissonant Quartet, not Schubert, under the mirror III.1.107+?
  • Whose truths are amplified by the sound design, and whose are drowned out?

The audience as judge, jury and Commission

On the surface. The audience watches a private trial.

Dorfman withholds the verdict at the moment of decision. The action freezes, the Mozart begins and a mirror descends between the actors and the audience III.1.107+; lights pick out spectators two or three at a time III.1.107+.

In the final scene Gerardo and Paulina sit facing the mirror with their backs to the spectators III.2, opening+, so the theatre audience and the concert audience are one. In this reading the audience is cast as the national public, the people to whom the Commission reports.

Questions to explore
  • Were you, by the mirror, hoping Paulina would shoot? What does that tell you?
  • How much certainty do you need before you would convict Roberto?

The question of guilt

On the surface. Is Roberto the doctor?

The case against him. Paulina’s bodily recognition II.1.74+; the Schubert cassette in his car I.4.1+; the Nietzsche he quotes I.2.22, which Paulina remembers her torturer quoting II.1.87; the clinical detail of the confession III.1.58; and the planted errors he corrected III.1.99.

The case for him. No document, witness or face supports the identification. He offers innocent reasons for the cassette II.2.21. The confession was coached II.2.30 and he says it was invented III.1.88; he says the corrected name came from Gerardo III.1.94. The audience never sees the correction itself.

It could be argued that the play does not convict Roberto so much as expose how collective trauma can distort justice. That is one reading; the play also gives Paulina the last look III.2.1+.

Questions to explore
  • List the evidence you would admit in a court and the evidence you believe. Are the lists the same?
  • Does Paulina need Roberto to be guilty, or to repent III.1.99?

Lens: Brecht and epic theatre

On the surface. The play looks like a conventional realist drama: one place, one night, three characters.

It could be argued that Dorfman uses Brechtian estrangement inside an apparently Aristotelian form. The refusal of a verdict, the music that comments rather than illustrates, and the mirror all stop the audience losing itself in the story and push it to judge.

Gerardo can be read as the audience’s representative: the reasonable liberal whose responses II.1.6 model how a well-meaning spectator thinks, and whose limits the play exposes. Dorfman also departs from Brecht: he uses the body and emotion as political instruments rather than keeping the audience at a distance from them.

Questions to explore
  • Is Death and the Maiden a Brechtian play, or a realist play with Brechtian moments?
  • Does the mirror estrange the audience, or implicate it emotionally?

Lens: Arendt and Kafka on the bureaucracy of justice

On the surface. The Commission is a good institution with limits.

Two ideas are useful here: Arendt’s idea of the banality of evil, harm done by people who stop thinking and follow process, and Kafka’s The Trial, where the law is inaccessible to the person it concerns. Paulina stands before an institution built for cases like hers whose remit excludes her I.1.64.

Roberto’s confession offers a test: it describes the doctor’s role in procedural, almost medical terms III.1.57+. This raises the question of whether impunity is produced by individual malice or by a structure that produces it without anyone intending it.

Questions to explore
  • If Gerardo’s role requires him to manage Paulina’s claim rather than advance it, is he her ally or the system’s agent?
  • Is the doctor in the confession a sadist, a functionary, or both?

Lens: Arendt and Voltaire on forgiveness, torture and anger

On the surface. The play asks whether Paulina should forgive.

Arendt’s account of forgiveness, which releases people from the consequences of past acts, can be set against Voltaire’s fury at torture and arbitrary power. Arendt helps read the demand for repentance III.1.99; Voltaire defends the victim’s anger as a rational response, not madness, which is how Gerardo and Roberto describe it II.2.39.

Questions to explore
  • Can forgiveness be demanded by a state, or only given by a victim?
  • Is Paulina’s anger reasonable? Who in the play says it is not, and why?

Lens: liberty, freedom and the individual against the state

On the surface. The country is now free.

Isaiah Berlin distinguishes negative liberty (freedom from interference) from positive liberty (the real capacity to act) and adds Pettit’s republican liberty (freedom from domination). Paulina is no longer imprisoned, yet Gerardo himself says she is still a prisoner II.1.79; the men who held her may still live freely around her II.1.80.

A related question asks whose needs come first when the state and the individual want different things. Gerardo puts the Commission first II.1.67; Paulina asks why people like her must always concede III.1.107.

Questions to explore
  • Is the end of active harm enough to call someone free?
  • Can a state that overrides the individual still claim to guarantee freedom?

Essential questions on justice

On the surface. Use these to open any discussion or essay plan.

What is the difference between justice and revenge? Who has the right to judge? Can truth exist without confession? What does a society owe its victims? How does power shape whose suffering is recognised? Is certainty necessary for justice? What role does memory play in the pursuit of justice? Can reconciliation exist without accountability? How does gender shape the experience and performance of justice? Is justice achievable in a fundamentally unjust system? When the state and the individual want different things, whose needs take precedence?
